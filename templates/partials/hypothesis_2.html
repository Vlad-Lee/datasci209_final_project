Hypothesis 2

Do neighborhoods with more tree canopy actually run cooler?

Every point in this section is a California census block group, measured against its own city's average temperature. Scroll down to see what the data shows.

Figure 1

The leafier the block, the cooler it runs.

Neil Sagoo. Average temperature difference from city average by tree canopy level, all 24,253 California block groups. Filter by EPA community designation or focus on a single metro area. Source: American Forests Tree Equity Score dataset.

The staircase is steady statewide. Block groups with almost no canopy run about 1.8°F hotter than their city average, and each step up in tree cover cools things further, down to about 4.7°F below the city average once canopy passes 60%. Canopy on its own only explains about 9% of the variation, so it is one real factor among several, not the whole story.

The community designation filter shows who this pattern falls hardest on. Isolate EPA-designated disadvantaged block groups and the whole staircase shifts upward: at every canopy level, disadvantaged blocks run hotter than non-designated ones, about 1.5°F hotter on average and almost 3°F in the barest neighborhoods. More trees narrow the gap, but they never fully close it. The same canopy does not deliver the same cooling everywhere.

Temperature is measured against each city's own average, not in absolute degrees, so compare blocks within a city rather than across cities.

So why does canopy cool a block? It comes down to shade, and specifically shade from trees and plants rather than from buildings. The chart below plots how much of each block's ground is shaded by vegetation against how hot the block runs, at three times of day. The shade data covers 15 of California's 187 urban areas (about 11,900 block groups), so this view focuses on the 11 metros with enough coverage to fit a real trend.

Figure 2

Explore it yourself. Switch the time of day, filter to a metro area, hover any point to read that block's shade and temperature, or use the highlighter to light up EPA-designated disadvantaged communities. The trend line and the slope in the title update with whatever you select.

Neil Sagoo. Vegetation shade vs. temperature difference at noon, 3 p.m., and 6 p.m., colored by tree canopy with the color scale centered at 16%, the statewide average. The trend slope recomputes for the selected time and metro. Source: American Forests Tree Equity Score dataset.

The slope is the story here. At noon, when the sun is high and tree shade does the most work, every 10 points of vegetation shade is worth about 1.7°F of cooling. By 3 p.m. it is still 1.6°F, and by evening the effect drops to about half a degree. Geography matters too. San Diego shows the steepest payoff, around 3°F per 10 points of shade at noon, with Los Angeles close behind. In San Francisco the relationship disappears entirely, because the marine layer cools every block whether it has trees or not. That matches the coastal anomaly in our redlining analysis (Hypothesis 3): the ocean can hide a heat disparity, but it does not undo the inequity underneath.

Finding: Shade from vegetation is how tree canopy turns into cooler neighborhoods, worth about 1.7°F per 10 points of shade at midday in California's major metros. But trees do not explain everything. At the same canopy level, EPA-designated disadvantaged communities still run about 1.5°F hotter than their neighbors. That sets up the next question: who actually lives in the hottest, least-shaded blocks.
