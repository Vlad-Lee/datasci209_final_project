Hypothesis 4

Socioeconomically Disadvantaged Neighborhoods Have Lower Tree Equity Scores

This section explores whether socioeconomically disadvantaged communities consistently experience lower Tree Equity Scores and identifies the environmental factors associated with these disparities through a series of coordinated visualizations.

Figure 1

The first dashboard was developed to establish where tree equity disparities are concentrated across California. We began with the county-level Tree Equity Score analysis from our exploratory work and expanded it into a side-by-side comparison of average Tree Equity Score and Priority Index. The Tree Equity Score provides a measure of existing tree equity, while the Priority Index helps identify communities where additional investment may be most needed. Interactive filters for disadvantaged status, population, HOLC grade, urban area, and county allow users to examine how these patterns change across different populations and geographic contexts.

The statewide pattern suggests that tree equity is not evenly distributed across California. Counties with lower average Tree Equity Scores also tend to show higher priority needs, indicating that communities with fewer existing tree benefits may simultaneously be those where additional investment is most warranted. Importantly, these differences become more meaningful when the dashboard is filtered to disadvantaged communities: the map allows users to see that disadvantage is not simply an individual characteristic, but is geographically concentrated in particular regions. This provides an important geographic context for the hypothesis and helps identify where disparities are most pronounced.

Figure 2

The second dashboard was developed to investigate what environmental conditions may contribute to these differences in tree equity. Our exploratory analysis showed a negative relationship between tree canopy and temperature difference, so we expanded this relationship into an interactive scatterplot. Each point represents a census block group, with tree canopy on the x-axis and temperature difference on the y-axis. The average values divide the plot into quadrants, allowing us to identify communities with relatively low canopy and high temperatures. Users can also change the metric used to color the points, making it possible to examine how Tree Equity Score, Priority Index, and other measures relate to these environmental conditions.

The relationship provides an important explanation for the broader disparity observed in Figure 1. Communities with less tree canopy tend to experience greater temperature disparities, meaning that disadvantaged communities with low tree equity may face a combination of fewer trees and greater exposure to heat. The relationship is not perfectly linear, however, and the substantial variation in temperature at similar levels of canopy demonstrates that tree canopy alone does not explain differences in heat exposure. Other factors such as built environment, geography, and local climate likely play important roles.

The final distribution in Figure 2 provides the clearest evidence for the hypothesis. Disadvantaged communities are shifted substantially toward lower Tree Equity Scores compared with non-disadvantaged communities. Non-disadvantaged communities have a much larger concentration of scores toward the upper end of the scale, including a notable concentration near 100, while disadvantaged communities are more concentrated around the mid-to-lower range. This difference suggests that the disparity is not driven simply by a few unusually low-scoring neighborhoods; rather, disadvantaged communities as a group experience systematically lower tree equity.

Overall, the data support Hypothesis 4. Across California, disadvantaged communities tend to have lower Tree Equity Scores than non-disadvantaged communities, while also exhibiting environmental characteristics associated with lower tree equity, including reduced tree canopy and greater temperature disparities. The county-level analysis demonstrates that these disparities are geographically concentrated, while the environmental analysis suggests that limited canopy and heat exposure are important components of the broader inequity.

However, these results should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of causation. The relatively weak relationship between tree canopy and temperature indicates that canopy is only one contributor to local heat conditions, and the Tree Equity Score itself incorporates several underlying socioeconomic and environmental factors. Thus, the findings are strongest as evidence of a systematic disparity in environmental conditions, rather than proof that socioeconomic disadvantage directly causes lower tree equity.
